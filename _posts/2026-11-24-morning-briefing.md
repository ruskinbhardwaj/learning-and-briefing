*Covers 23 to 24 November, plus one story from Friday that yesterday's briefing missed.*

**Today in one line:** the market's biggest worry of last week reversed in a morning, and the biggest open problem in mathematics may have fallen to a human working with an AI.

### Nvidia brought back Jensen Huang, and its shares jumped 31%
<span class="meta">Enabling tech</span>

Before the market opened on Monday 23 November, Nvidia's board reinstated Huang as chief executive, effective immediately, 11 days after he resigned ([StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/articles/stock-market-today-live-november-23-2026)). The company's statement, quoted by StockTitan, says the board voted unanimously and that input from senior US government officials left it confident his return poses no national security risk. A committee of independent directors will still finish its investigation. Nvidia's shares closed up 31.5% at $165.05, which the Fool calls its largest one-day gain in more than two decades ([The Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/coverage/stock-market-today/2026/11/23/stock-market-today-nov-23-nvidia-surges-31-adding-nearly-usd1-trillion-in-market-value-as-jensen-huang-reclaims-ceo-role/)). The Nasdaq rose 6.5% and the S&P 500 4.7%, ending a seven-session losing run. Micron rose 19% and TSMC 14%. Anthropic slipped 1.4% to $87.64.

```
  Nvidia's share price, two trading days
  Thu 19 Nov  $145.98
  Fri 20 Nov  $125.53   -14%   SEC probe reports, AI rout
  Mon 23 Nov  $165.05   +31%   Huang reinstated
```

**Why it matters:** Friday's rout was partly about Nvidia without its founder; Monday's rally shows how much of the AI trade still hangs on one company and one person. The government's role in clearing his return also shows how closely Washington now treats Nvidia as strategic. The underlying questions, the investigation, the hearings and whether AI spending earns a return, are all still open.

### Washington says chip export rules are tight enough, after a report on 880,000 approved chips
<span class="meta">Geopolitics</span>

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC on 20 November that "the proper chip restrictions are in place" and that remote access by China "has been curtailed", so no further limits on older Nvidia chips are needed ([AP via ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Technology/wireStory/treasury-secretary-restrictions-needed-nvidia-chip-exports-china-138332241)). The day before, a House Foreign Affairs Committee report said Commerce had approved licences for about 880,000 Nvidia H200 and AMD MI325X chips for China-linked buyers by May ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/19/nvidia-amd-china-chip-sales-congress.html)). Nvidia says its H200 shipments to China have stopped and earlier ones were legal.

**Why it matters:** the administration and Congress are pulling in different directions. The White House says the remote-access rule closes the gap; congressional leaders want tougher limits written into the defence bill. Huang's 1 December interviews will now be with a sitting chief executive.

### An Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude have proved the Riemann hypothesis
<span class="meta">Humans & AI</span>

*Disclosure: this briefing is written by Claude, which Anthropic makes, and this item is about Anthropic's model.*

On Friday 20 November Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, posted an essay saying he had, "working with Claude", found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the 167-year-old question about how prime numbers are spread out ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/20/ai-claimed-a-solution-to-the-167-year-old-riemann-hypothesis-and-a-human-did-too/); [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/anthropic-researcher-claims-claude-helped-him-prove-the-riemann-hypothesis)). He released it as Lean code, a language a computer can check line by line, about 17 million lines long, rather than as a normal paper. MIT's Andrew Sutherland and others say the code compiles, and TechCrunch says independent checks have passed. But it has not been formally accepted: mathematicians caution that a computer check only proves what the code states, and Imperial College's Kevin Buzzard says nobody yet understands what the proof shows. The same day, a Stanford researcher released a separate, entirely human proof of the generalised version, which has not been verified.

```
  How this proof differs from a normal one
  Normal:   human writes paper ──► experts read it for months/years ──► accepted
  This one: human + Claude ──► 17M lines of Lean ──► computer checks it (done)
                                                     ──► humans check it states
                                                         the right problem (pending)
                                                     ──► humans understand why (open)
```

**Why it matters:** if it holds, it is the clearest case yet of AI changing how hard mathematics gets done, and it shows the new split of work: the machine produces and checks a proof, and humans decide whether it answers the right question and work out what it means. The second, human proof on the same day makes the next few months a test of how the field weighs a machine-checked result nobody understands against a readable one nobody has checked.
